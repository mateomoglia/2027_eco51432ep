Debugging clinic

Roadmap

This is the part of the course that justifies the rest of it. We are going to:

  • Find four mistakes in a script that runs perfectly
  • Check that your own project is reproducible, by having someone else run it

You have spent two sessions learning verbs you could have got from a chatbot in four seconds. What follows is the part a chatbot cannot do for you, because it requires knowing what your variables mean.

Why this exists

An LLM is very good at R syntax and completely indifferent to your research question. It will produce code that runs, that looks idiomatic, that even looks careful, and that answers a question you did not ask.

Three of the four mistakes below are things I have seen in submitted work. The fourth I have made myself.

The taxonomy

Loud failures stop your code. An error message appears, you read it, you fix it, five minutes gone. These are your friends.

Silent failures do not stop anything. Your code runs, your table fills in, your figure looks plausible, and your number is wrong. Nothing will ever tell you. Every mistake below is of this kind, which is why the exercise is hard.

The four families you have now met, and which cover most of what you will hit in practice:

  1. Joins: the wrong key, an unspecified key, or a key with a formatting mismatch
  2. Missing values: dropped silently, or coded as 99999 and treated as a number
  3. Formats: a decimal separator misread, a numeric column read as text, a leading zero lost
  4. Units and aggregation: an unweighted mean passed off as a population mean, or a ratio of two things that should never have been divided

Exercise: four mistakes

Here is a script. I asked an LLM for “code that computes average rents and population by département from these two files”, and this is roughly what came back. It runs from top to bottom without a single error or warning.

It contains four mistakes. None of them throws anything. Find them.

#-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
# Average rent and population by département, Ile-de-France
#-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

library(dplyr)

path = "C:/Users/mateomoglia/Dropbox/courses/polytechnique/2027_eco51432ep"

pop   = read.csv2(paste0(path, "/raw_data/idf_pop.csv"))
rents = read.csv2(paste0(path, "/raw_data/idf_rents.csv"))

df = pop %>%
  left_join(rents) %>%
  mutate(dep           = substr(codgeo, 1, 2),
         rent_per_head = rent_appartement / population) %>%
  group_by(dep) %>%
  summarise(mean_rent     = mean(rent_appartement),
            total_pop     = sum(population),
            mean_per_head = mean(rent_per_head))

df
How to work on it

Do not read the script looking for something that “looks wrong”. Nothing looks wrong, that is the point. Instead, work in this order:

  1. For every line, write down what you expect the object to look like afterwards: how many rows, which columns, what range of values.
  2. Run the script one line at a time and compare.
  3. For the final table, ask the economist’s question rather than the programmer’s one: if I published this number, what exactly would I be claiming?

Work in pairs. Give yourselves twenty minutes. Finding three of the four is a good result.

Solution

Mistake 1: the join has no key.

left_join(rents)

dplyr guesses the key from every column the two tables share. It prints Joining with by = join_by(codgeo) and everyone ignores it. If both files also contain a libelle or a year column, you have silently joined on two keys, and every municipality whose label is spelled differently in the two files has disappeared. Nothing tells you.

left_join(rents, by = "codgeo")

Then check, always:

nrow(pop); nrow(df)
pop %>% anti_join(rents, by = "codgeo") %>% nrow()

Mistake 2: no na.rm, or rather, no decision about missing values.

mean(rent_appartement) returns NA for any département containing a single municipality with a missing rent. That is the loud version, and it is the good outcome, because you notice.

The bad outcome is what most people do next, which is to add na.rm = TRUE because they wanted a number and now they have one. That silently redefines the statistic as “the average rent among municipalities that happen to report one”, which is a different object and probably a selected one, since small rural communes are the ones missing. The fix is not a keystroke, it is a decision you document:

summarise(mean_rent = mean(rent_appartement, na.rm = TRUE),
          n_missing = sum(is.na(rent_appartement)))

Report n_missing in the table. If it is 2 out of 500, fine. If it is 200 out of 500, you have a different paper.

Mistake 3: an unweighted mean reported as a departmental average.

mean(rent_appartement) averages across municipalities, giving a village of 300 people the same weight as a commune of 90,000. The resulting number is the average rent of the average municipality, which is not the average rent faced by the average resident, and the two differ substantially in Ile-de-France, where population and rents are both concentrated.

Neither is wrong in the abstract. Reporting one under a label that means the other is wrong. If you want the population-weighted version:

summarise(mean_rent = weighted.mean(rent_appartement, population, na.rm = TRUE))

This is the mistake an LLM will make every single time, because “average rent by département” is ambiguous in English and unambiguous only once you know what the row of your data represents. It cannot know that. You can.

Mistake 4: rent_per_head is not a quantity.

rent_appartement is a rent per square metre, in euros. population is a headcount. Their ratio has units of euros per square metre per person, which corresponds to nothing in the world. It is not a rent burden, it is not an affordability index, it is not anything. The variable name makes it sound meaningful, and mean_per_head in the final table makes it look like a result.

This one throws nothing, plots beautifully, and would survive a referee who reads quickly. It is the reason this course exists: R will divide any two columns you point at, and only you know whether the result means anything.

If you want a rent burden, you need rent paid over income, which requires the income variable and a decision about dwelling size. That is a modelling choice, not a mutate().

Two more things the script does not do, which are not among the four but which you should have noticed:

  • No str() after either import, so nobody has checked that rent_appartement is numeric and that codgeo is character. If codgeo was read as a number, substr() still works here because Ile-de-France départements start at 75, but the same code silently breaks on 01004.
  • No sessionInfo() at the end, so in a year nobody will know which package versions produced this table.

Exercise: reproducibility

This one takes ten minutes and is more informative than it sounds.

Swap and run
  1. Zip your project folder, the whole thing, and send it to the person next to you.
  2. Unzip theirs somewhere on your machine.
  3. Change the single path line at the top of their script.
  4. Restart R (Session > Restart R) and run their script from the first line to the last.
  5. Write down every place it broke.

Then swap the list of breakages back. That list is your homework, and it is also exactly the email you will get from a referee or a co-author in two years, except that in two years it will be about your own code and you will have forgotten everything.

Common findings: a package loaded in the console but never in the script, an object created by hand and never saved, a file read from Downloads, a hardcoded year, an /output folder that does not exist so ggsave fails, and data files that were never in the zip at all.

A closing note

None of this is about distrusting the tools. Use them. They are better at R syntax than I am and they are certainly better than you need them to be.

What they cannot do is know that rent_appartement is measured per square metre, that your municipalities are not equally sized, that 99999 means missing in this file and not in that one, or that the referee will ask about the 40 communes you dropped. That knowledge is the job. The code was never the job.
